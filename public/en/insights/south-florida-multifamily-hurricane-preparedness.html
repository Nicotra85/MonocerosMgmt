Hurricane Preparedness for South Florida Multifamily Properties

A storm plan is useful only when the team can execute it. For multifamily owners, preparation means assigning decisions, organizing communications and knowing which records will be needed when normal operations are interrupted.

September 22, 2026 · 3 min read

Prepare before the forecast becomes urgent

The National Weather Service recommends advance planning around evacuation zones, emergency supplies, communications and insurance coverage. Use its hurricane preparedness guidance alongside instructions from local emergency officials. A property plan should support those instructions, not substitute for them.

Build a responsibility chart naming the primary decision-maker and a backup for each property. Agree on who communicates with residents, who authorizes emergency vendor work and who updates ownership. Record the spending limits and escalation contacts before a storm is approaching.

Create a practical building-readiness register

Ask the maintenance team and qualified vendors to identify property-specific preparation tasks and document completion. Organize open repair issues by building so recurring water intrusion or equipment concerns do not disappear inside a general checklist.

Maintain current contact details for critical vendors and confirm what their emergency service agreements actually cover. A phone number alone is not a response commitment. Record alternatives when a vendor may serve many affected properties at once, and establish a safe process for reporting conditions without asking staff to enter hazardous areas.

Communicate clearly with residents

Prepare short messages in the languages used at the community. Explain the management contact channel, how residents can report damage and where to find official emergency instructions. Do not promise that management can provide emergency rescue or continuous building services.

Keep contact information current and plan an alternative communication method if the normal channel is unavailable. Avoid distributing sensitive resident information broadly. Document when notices were issued so ownership can understand the communication timeline after the event.

Prepare the financial and insurance records

Review coverage, deductibles, reporting procedures and documentation requirements with the insurance professional responsible for the property. Do not assume one policy covers every type of loss. Maintain accessible copies of policies, vendor agreements, recent condition photographs and the current asset inventory.

Set up a dedicated incident cost code before work begins. Separating emergency mitigation, ordinary maintenance and longer-term restoration makes later reconciliation easier. Preserve authorizations, invoices and supporting photographs in an organized record rather than relying on a chain of text messages.

Structure the return to normal operations

Follow official access restrictions and qualified assessments before sending teams back into affected areas. Once access is safe, use a consistent condition-reporting template by building and unit. Distinguish verified findings from resident reports still awaiting review.

Ownership updates should state service interruptions, confirmed damage, work authorized, cash needs and the next decision. After the response, conduct a brief review: which contact failed, which record was missing and which task took longer than expected? Update the plan while those lessons are fresh.
